Idea 003 · Symbolic AI · Decision model distillation

CAN WE COMPILE EXPERTISE?

From rule engines and knowledge graphs to neural decision models: can decades of explicitly encoded human expertise become efficient, probabilistic decision-making systems without losing their reliability?

The hypothesis: Legacy expert systems and structured knowledge bases can serve as unusually precise supervision for specialized neural decision models, preserving much of their domain decision behavior while improving robustness to messy inputs and deployment flexibility.

The overlooked dataset

Long before modern language models, researchers and organizations encoded domain knowledge as production rules, Prolog programs, decision tables, ontologies, knowledge graphs, and expert-system shells. Unlike unstructured web text, many of these artifacts can provide explicit premises, conclusions, exceptions, provenance and machine-executable decisions. They are not automatically suitable training corpora: a knowledge graph alone does not specify a decision policy, and some rule bases are incomplete, proprietary or outdated.

Why now: Cloudflare Clef

Cloudflare introduced Clef and Clef-flash in October 2026, open-weight decision models built to return probabilities over schema-constrained choices instead of free-form prose. Their Apache-2.0-licensed weights offer a concrete experimental target. Clef is a 27-billion-parameter model; Clef-flash is a smaller 9-billion-parameter alternative. Both are post-trained from language-model backbones, not symbolic inference engines, and they do not inherently guarantee compliance with hard rules.

Primary research questions

  1. Behavioral distillation: Can queries to a deterministic or probabilistic legacy expert system produce state–question–answer training examples that successfully adapt Clef or Clef-flash?
  2. Fidelity: How accurately does a trained model reproduce the original system's decisions on unseen combinations of facts, exceptions, and rule conflicts?
  3. Data efficiency: How many synthetic examples are required? Does coverage-guided rule sampling outperform random case generation?
  4. Uncertainty: Can a neural student be calibrated to abstain or escalate when inputs fall outside the expert system's modeled domain?
  5. Knowledge graph transfer: Can graph-derived facts and constraints be converted into decision supervision without collapsing multi-hop relations or provenance?
  6. Maintenance: How cheaply can the student be updated when source rules change, and can outdated behavior be reliably removed?

A concrete research program

01 / Source

Choose an executable expert

Start with an openly licensed rule engine, Prolog knowledge base or decision-table corpus. Verify licenses, reproducibility, rule coverage and ability to query it as an oracle.

02 / Generate

Turn rules into cases

Construct input states and typed questions; execute the symbolic oracle to label outcomes. Oversample rare branches, contradictions, exceptions, missing values and boundary conditions.

03 / Train

Adapt a decision model

Compare prompt-only Clef, fine-tuned Clef-flash, conventional classifiers and a hybrid system that consults the original rules. Establish the available fine-tuning implementation before committing compute.

04 / Stress-test

Find the breaking points

Use unseen combinations, distribution shifts, adversarial exceptions and rule updates. Measure exact decision agreement, constraint violations, calibration, latency, cost and abstention quality.

Key experimental controls

Compare against direct execution of the original expert system, which may already be cheap, exact and auditable. Hold out entire rule combinations rather than merely random generated cases. Test both deterministic answers and uncertainty-aware choices; a probabilistic model is not automatically an improvement over an exact rule engine. Report cases where the neural student generalizes usefully and cases where it hallucinates or violates hard constraints.

Possible dissertation contributions

A reproducible compiler-like pipeline from symbolic decision specifications to schema-constrained neural supervision; a benchmark for rule-fidelity and generalization under sparse coverage; and an empirical map of when distillation, direct symbolic execution or hybrid neuro-symbolic systems is the better engineering choice.

Feasibility and novelty

The first prototype could use a small, publicly licensed ruleset and a few thousand generated cases, but these are starting conditions rather than validated requirements. Fine-tuning the 9B model may require rented GPU resources depending on the method. The general idea overlaps with established work on neuro-symbolic learning, rule distillation, program synthesis and learning from executable teachers; a literature review is necessary before claiming novelty. A stronger thesis would identify a specific contribution in coverage-aware sampling, provable constraints, calibrated abstention, or faithful updates.

Foundations and references

The bigger question: Can the explicit reasoning machinery of an earlier AI era become a training signal for a new generation of compact decision models—without sacrificing the correctness that made symbolic systems valuable?

Prior work: what is already known?

West et al., Symbolic Knowledge Distillation (NAACL 2022) explicitly studies knowledge distillation involving symbolic commonsense representations. Its direction is not identical to executing a legacy expert-system oracle and supervising a schema-constrained decision model, but it establishes that “symbolic distillation” is not a new category. Classic rule-to-network transfer, neuro-symbolic learning, learning from programs, and knowledge-graph embedding must also be covered in a systematic novelty review. The potentially defensible contribution is a reproducible method and benchmark for preserving executable rule behavior, rare exceptions and updateability in decision models—not merely training on rule-generated labels.

Concrete source systems and reusable code

CLIPS — historical starting point

CLIPS official project is a NASA-origin production-rule expert-system language, with public-domain engine and documented examples. It provides an executable oracle and authentic older expert-system syntax. First step: select a nontrivial example and enumerate facts, fired rules and final decisions.

PyKnow — Python rule examples

PyKnow GitHub contains CLIPS-inspired Python rules, including traffic-light examples. Its LGPL-3.0 license and age should be considered; the small demos are useful for pipeline validation, not enough alone for a dissertation benchmark.

OpenRules — decision tables

OpenRules sample catalog describes 50+ sample decision projects, including loan and vacation decisions. Useful as more realistic structured rule specifications, but do not assume the samples or runtime are open-source; verify redistribution and execution rights before using them.

SWI-Prolog — declarative rules

SWI-Prolog provides an established executable logic-programming environment. Identify a separately licensed Prolog rule corpus and wrap it as a queryable teacher; the Prolog interpreter itself is not a decision dataset.

Cloudflare Clef: implementation details verified

Cloudflare's October 1, 2026 technical announcement describes Clef (Qwen3.8-27B backbone) and Clef-flash (Qwen3.5-9B backbone). The model receives a state and typed questions, then scores allowed answer choices with a joint schema head; it does not produce free-form text. Cloudflare reports freezing the backbone while training a routing head plus rank-256 LoRA adapters, with label-smoothed cross-entropy, Brier calibration loss and an additional RL objective. The released joint_schema_model.py implements record encoding, batching, the head, checkpoint loading and inference. That is not a complete published training recipe. Reproducing the post-training objective and trainable-parameter setup may require custom PyTorch code. Cloudflare's managed fine-tuning service launched initially as a design-partner offering, not an independently verified self-service workflow.

Compute planning: estimates, not benchmarks

Clef-flash's 9B parameters require roughly 18 GB just for BF16 weights, before activations, KV cache, vision components and the schema head. Full-precision optimizer training would require much more; a naive Adam setup can exceed 100 GB before activations. A first head-only experiment with frozen backbone should be profiled on a rented 48–80 GB accelerator, depending on sequence length and batch size. A LoRA-plus-head experiment is more demanding and an 80 GB GPU is a safer initial profiling target, not a guaranteed fit. Multi-GPU training or additional memory optimizations may be needed. The 27B Clef backbone alone requires approximately 54 GB in BF16 and is not the economical first target.

For an initial cost-controlled pilot, run the source oracle locally, generate 1,000–10,000 labeled decision records, test inference through Cloudflare's hosted Clef-flash API, and then rent a GPU for a small reproducible training benchmark. Measure actual peak VRAM, examples/second and convergence before projecting costs for 100,000+ examples. No verified public Clef-flash fine-tuning GPU-hour benchmark has been established here.

First reproducible milestone

  1. Run a CLIPS or PyKnow rule base as the ground-truth decision oracle.
  2. Generate structured states, typed options and labels; explicitly enumerate exception paths and conflict cases.
  3. Evaluate untuned Clef-flash, a simple tabular classifier and direct rule execution on identical held-out cases.
  4. Implement and profile head-only or LoRA-plus-head adaptation from the released model code.
  5. Test new rule combinations, changed rules, abstention, constraint violations, latency and GPU-hour cost.

Decision gate: If a neural model cannot outperform a direct rule engine on any meaningful robustness, interoperability or latency criterion, the scientifically useful result may be that preserving symbolic execution is preferable. The dissertation should report that outcome rather than assume distillation is beneficial.
