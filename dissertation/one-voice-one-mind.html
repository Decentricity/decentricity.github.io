Idea 001 · Audio-native AI · Computational identity

ONE VOICE.
ONE MIND?

Can a neural network trained on the speech of one human learn not merely their voice, but the recognizable patterns of their conversational personality—without ever reading a transcript?

The central experiment: What happens when we replace the anonymous crowd of voices behind a general speech model with a deep, longitudinal record of one person's life in conversation?

Why this matters

Text-based replicas learn from emails, posts and documents: deliberate, edited projections of a person. Real speech also contains interruptions, hesitation, laughter, prosody, code-switching, spontaneous choices and relationships. A truly audio-native model might capture behavioral signatures that text misses. Whether it does is a testable hypothesis, not an established fact.

Research questions

  1. Single-speaker specialization: Can a textless speech-to-speech architecture learn stable conversational behavior from one focal speaker, while retaining the ability to respond to other speakers?
  2. Data efficiency: What changes with 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1,000 hours of consented recordings? Where do improvements plateau?
  3. Synthetic expansion: Can 10–100 hours of genuine audio be augmented with generated speech to improve speaker and behavioral fidelity, or does it simply reinforce synthetic artifacts?
  4. Behavior versus voice: Can listeners identify the person when voice timbre is normalized? Does the model make similar decisions in held-out conversations?
  5. Textlessness: How does an audio-token-only student compare with transcription-based and audio-plus-text baselines under matched budgets?
  6. Generalization: Does the model preserve humor, discourse style and conversational preferences across unfamiliar topics and interlocutors rather than memorize rehearsed responses?

Data acquisition: an experiment within reach

Recruit one consenting focal speaker—potentially the researcher or a paid participant—for longitudinal collection. Use a high-quality wearable microphone during scheduled conversations, interviews, storytelling, debates and unscripted daily speech. Record conversation partners only with their informed consent; avoid covert recording and collect withdrawal permissions. An intern could be a compensated speaker, but 1,000 hours of useful conversational audio requires extensive scheduling, quality control and breaks, not merely continuous monologue.

Separate genuine interaction, solo speech, prompted dialogues and synthetic audio. Preserve session boundaries, conversational context and audio quality metadata. Hold out entire days, topics and interlocutors for evaluation to prevent leakage.

Possible study design

Phase 1

Establish a textless baseline

Reproduce an audio-native conversational model such as Meta's dGSLM; evaluate code, checkpoint availability and compute requirements before committing to an architecture.

Phase 2

One-person adaptation

Compare a single-speaker model, a multispeaker baseline and a text-conditioned baseline. Use matched compute and identical held-out dialogue tasks.

Phase 3

Augmentation study

Vary real-to-synthetic ratios, generated dialogue diversity and audio quality. Report whether synthetic expansion helps beyond simply replaying real recordings.

Phase 4

Blind identity tests

Use human raters familiar with the participant, independent raters, and measurable turn-taking/prosody statistics. Test behavioral similarity separately from vocal similarity.

How to measure success

Pre-register speaker similarity, response latency, interruption handling, prosodic distributions, dialogue coherence, consistency on repeated novel scenarios, privacy leakage and human blind-choice identification. Compare performance at fixed training-data and GPU-hour budgets. Include negative results and ablations: synthetic-only, real-only, shuffled speaker labels and transcripts-only.

Feasibility and risks

Fine-tuning a pretrained audio model may be tractable on rented accelerators, but training a high-quality textless full-duplex system from scratch is a substantially larger undertaking. The proposed hour counts are experimental conditions, not guaranteed sufficiency thresholds. Continuous recording creates substantial consent, security and privacy obligations. A convincing behavioral simulation is not evidence of transferred memories, consciousness or mind uploading.

Foundational work

dGSLM: Generative Spoken Dialogue Language Modeling (TACL, 2023) — a textless spoken-dialogue research baseline. Research code. Kyutai Moshi — a useful modern full-duplex comparison, although it is not strictly text-free.

Possible dissertation contribution: A reproducible, controlled study of whether audio-native, single-person training improves the measurable preservation of conversational behavior, and how little authentic speech is needed.

Parallel research question: can speech-to-speech models be extracted?

Alongside the primary study of single-person conversational identity, a related cybersecurity experiment could ask: Is a black-box model extraction or distillation attack possible against a commercial speech-to-speech system? For example, could a student trained on permitted query–response audio from a voice service such as ElevenLabs reproduce measurable properties of the original system without access to its parameters or text representations?

This is a separate, optional research track rather than a third dissertation proposal. It could provide a synthetic-audio baseline or comparison for the single-person model: how much of the apparent conversational identity is attributable to copying a voice generator, versus learning authentic human conversational behavior? A useful null hypothesis would be that synthetic teacher outputs provide no measurable improvement in held-out behavioral fidelity over a matched real-audio-only baseline.

Any commercial-system experiment would first require checking applicable terms, permissions and research ethics; an explicitly licensed open model could serve as a reproducible initial target. The investigation would distinguish voice imitation from extraction of actual speech-to-speech conversational behavior.
