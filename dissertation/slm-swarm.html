Idea 005 · Swarm intelligence · Heterogeneous model networks

Swarm Coordination of Small Language Models for Domain-Specific Assistance

Can a swarm of small conversational and decision models coordinate like a hive to become a capable narrow-use assistant—and when should its members cooperate, specialize, or compete?

Research feasibility · preliminary estimates

Compute & corpus requirements

Corpus: Begin with 100–300 manually checked tasks in one bounded domain, such as technical support for a documented software product. Expand to 1,000–5,000 cases covering ambiguous requests, missing facts, rare exceptions, conflicting evidence and escalation. Split by scenario family and document version, rather than paraphrases alone. These are proposed study sizes, not established sufficiency thresholds.

Compute: Start with 3–5 logical roles using existing small chat models and a compact decision model. An illustrative chat-model range is 1–4B parameters; define and report the actual size of every model, including routers and judges. A 12–24 GB GPU is a plausible pilot target for sequential quantized inference, subject to context length, runtime and profiling. Logical roles need not be separate resident models. Larger decision-model candidates such as Clef-flash require separate resource accounting and should not silently redefine the small-model budget.

Budget: At a cap of eight calls per task, 300 tasks require up to 2,400 calls per experimental condition, excluding offline evaluation. Multiply by conditions and repeated seeds; measure generated and input tokens, GPU-seconds, peak memory and end-to-end latency before projecting cost. Parallel execution may reduce latency while increasing total compute. Initial experiments use inference; adaptation and joint training are later, separately budgeted studies.

Research hypothesis: For a bounded assistant task, a heterogeneous swarm of small chat and decision models can improve task success per unit of compute over a single small model or a homogeneous model network through specialization, selective communication and evidence-based arbitration. Whether competition adds value is a separate, testable hypothesis.

The hive coordination analogy

A hive distributes work among specialized participants, communicates locally and responds collectively to changing conditions. This proposal uses that analogy to investigate division of labor, shared state, selective recruitment and collective decisions in model networks. “Swarm” names the coordination problem; it does not imply consciousness, biological equivalence or guaranteed emergent intelligence.

Compare centrally routed coordination with decentralized consultation and a shared case record. A centralized router is one experimental baseline, not an assumption that a hive requires a single controller. Models may run on one workstation initially; distribution across physical devices is a later systems experiment.

Two kinds of model, complementary roles

01 / Conversation

Small chat models

Interpret requests, ask clarifying questions, retrieve and explain domain information, and propose solutions. Specialists can focus on different subproblems. Record which facts came from the user, documentation or model inference.

02 / Decisions

Small decision models

Score bounded choices: consult a specialist, request missing evidence, select an action, initiate a challenge, stop or escalate. Distinguish a trained decision head from a chat model merely prompted to emit JSON. Calibrate probabilities and measure abstention; structured output alone does not establish correctness.

03 / Shared state

The case record

Exchange typed facts, source references, uncertainties, proposed actions and unresolved disagreements. Preserve provenance and allow corrections. Compare this record with full conversation histories and short natural-language messages.

04 / Collective response

Selection and explanation

A decision mechanism selects a supported next step; a conversational model explains it. Check whether the explanation preserves the selected action and its limitations. Use explicit domain constraints and a sandbox for executable actions.

PvP: models competing with one another

PvP is model-versus-model competition: two or more specialists independently propose candidate answers or action plans, then challenge competing claims with evidence or counterexamples. A decision model arbitrates among candidates, asks for more evidence, or abstains. Competition is an optional coordination mode within the swarm, alongside cooperation.

  1. Independent proposals: Each competitor sees the same case and permitted evidence, but not its opponent’s initial answer. This reduces immediate copying without assuming independent errors.
  2. Bounded challenge: Allow one or a small fixed number of rebuttal rounds. Require challenges to identify a disputed claim, supporting source or executable check, and the consequence for the proposed action.
  3. Evidence-based arbitration: Select an answer using a decision model and available task checks. Permit “neither answer is supported,” clarification and escalation. Randomize candidate order and hide model identity where possible.
  4. External scoring: Evaluate the chosen action against ground truth, sandbox outcomes or blinded human judgments. A model winning a debate is not evidence that it is correct.

Compare cooperative discussion, independent proposals without debate, majority voting, best-of-N sampling and PvP with the same total inference allowance. Test whether competition catches errors or instead rewards persuasive wording, increases correlated mistakes, or causes a judge to reject a correct minority answer. Track judge bias, positional effects, disagreement quality and extra latency. Competitive training or self-play can be a later extension; the initial study concerns inference-time competition.

Which network structure works best?

Fixed pipeline

Use a predetermined sequence of interpretation, specialist consultation, decision and explanation. Establish a simple reproducible baseline.

Central decision router

Recruit only the specialists needed for a case. Test whether routing saves enough computation to justify its own cost and errors.

Shared-state swarm

Let models contribute to a common case record under explicit update and stopping rules. Test whether indirect coordination reduces duplication or propagates incorrect facts.

Selective peer consultation

Allow bounded requests between specialists and optional PvP challenges. Measure communication overhead, loops and whether additional consultation changes the final outcome.

Core research questions

  1. Heterogeneity: Do dedicated decision models improve routing, action selection and escalation compared with chat-only roles?
  2. Communication: What must messages retain for reliable coordination, and what can be compressed without losing decisive evidence?
  3. Topology: Which tasks benefit from a fixed pipeline, central routing, shared state or peer consultation?
  4. Cooperation versus competition: When does PvP correct errors beyond what extra independent samples or cooperative critique achieve?
  5. Adaptive participation: Can the swarm stop early on easy cases and recruit additional models only when the expected benefit exceeds the cost?
  6. Failure propagation: Can downstream models detect an incorrect case summary, stale fact or misleading peer message instead of confidently repeating it?

Experimental design and essential controls

Choose one domain with documented procedures and verifiable outcomes. For technical support, a case might move from a user’s symptom report to a clarification, diagnosis and tested repair proposal in a sandbox. Hold tools, retrieval corpus and allowed actions constant across systems. Use a staged experiment: first test heterogeneous roles in a fixed topology, then communication formats, then PvP and adaptive routing. Avoid changing every component simultaneously.

  • Single-model baselines: A single small model with the same retrieval and tools, including repeated sampling or self-review under the swarm’s budget; a larger model as a quality and cost reference.
  • Network baselines: Chat-only roles, deterministic routing, a fixed heterogeneous pipeline and a cooperative swarm.
  • Competition controls: Independent candidates with selection, voting and best-of-N, alongside bounded adversarial debate. Use both shared-backbone and different-backbone competitors to study error correlation.
  • Ablations: Remove the decision model, provenance, specialization or challenge stage one at a time. Count router, judge, synthesis and unsuccessful calls in the total deployment budget.
  • Budget matching: Compare quality across measured compute budgets and latency constraints. Token counts alone are not equivalent compute across model sizes. Report model loading, communication and memory costs separately.

How to measure success

Pre-register task completion, consequential decision errors, clarification quality, calibration, escalation reliability and evidence support. Measure end-to-end latency, total GPU-seconds, tokens, peak memory and communication volume. Report uncertainty across repeated runs and task families, and show the quality–cost frontier rather than one favorable aggregate score.

Stress-test missing information, contradictory documents, incorrect shared-state entries, unavailable specialists and misleading retrieved content. Keep evaluation labels separate from routing and judge training. Use executable outcomes where possible and blinded human review where necessary; do not rely solely on the swarm grading itself.

What would falsify the hypotheses?

The main hypothesis is weakened if a single small model with equal resources matches or exceeds the swarm, if decision-model routing costs more than it saves, or if coordination propagates errors faster than it corrects them. The PvP hypothesis fails for a task class if debate adds no benefit over independent proposals, degrades accuracy or creates unacceptable latency. A useful negative result would identify where simple systems outperform collective coordination.

Prior art and possible contribution

Swarm intelligence, multi-agent systems, ensemble selection, mixture-of-experts routing and language-model debate are established research areas. A network of separate communicating models is not automatically equivalent to a mixture-of-experts layer inside one model. The hive analogy alone is not a novelty claim.

The possible dissertation contribution is a reproducible benchmark and experimentally supported coordination method for heterogeneous small chat and decision models, including a map of when cooperation, competition and selective communication improve narrow-domain assistance under resource constraints. A systematic literature review must establish the gap before claiming originality.

First milestone

Run 100 checked cases with a small conversational model, two competing specialist roles and a decision role, initially executed sequentially on one workstation. Compare a single-model baseline, cooperative coordination and one-round PvP. Log every message, decision, source and model call. Determine whether gains survive equal-compute comparisons before adding distributed hardware or training new models.

The bigger question: Can a hive of modest, specialized models deliver a dependable assistant through the way its members communicate, divide work and challenge one another?

Research & implementation starting points

  • AutoGen: Enabling Next-Gen LLM Applications via Multi-Agent Conversation — a starting point for reviewing model coordination.
  • Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate — relevant prior work for the PvP comparison.
  • QwenLM — candidate open-weight conversational models; verify each model’s size and license.
  • Cloudflare Clef-flash — a schema-constrained decision-model reference; its size and training requirements need separate evaluation.
  • llama.cpp and vLLM — candidate inference runtimes.

These are literature and implementation starting points, not evidence that the proposed swarm outperforms its baselines. Verify current licenses, model availability and reproducibility before experiments.
